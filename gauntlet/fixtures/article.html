Kestrel Ridge Observatory marks a quiet anniversary

High on the basalt shoulder above Carrow Valley, a telescope that almost never got built has spent three decades quietly rewriting what we know about variable stars.

The Kestrel Ridge Observatory was conceived in 1989 by Dr. Imogen Hale, an astronomer at the University of Carrow who had spent years arguing that the valley's unusually stable air column made it one of the best seeing sites in the hemisphere. Funding collapsed twice. Construction finally began in 1994.

After a delay caused by a cracked primary mirror blank, the observatory first came online in 1997, with a 2.4-metre reflector and an instrument package assembled largely from parts scavenged from a decommissioned facility in Torrance.

Its first decade was unglamorous. The staff, never more than eleven people, catalogued eclipsing binaries. But the long, patient baseline turned out to be the point: by 2011, the Kestrel Ridge catalogue was the longest continuous photometric record of its kind, and it underpinned the discovery of period drift in a class of stars that had been assumed stable.

Today the observatory is run jointly with the Marisan Institute, which took over operations in 2016 after Dr. Hale's retirement. Its current director, Dr. Peter Okonjo, has pushed the site toward automated survey work, a shift some of the original staff resented.

"People imagine astronomy is about the dramatic night," Okonjo said. "It is mostly about showing up for thirty years."

The observatory does not publish its operating budget, and the Marisan Institute declined to discuss funding for this article.
